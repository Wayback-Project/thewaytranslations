# Writing about The Way Version

**Applies to:** README, website, About page, articles, notes, release descriptions, and translations-method communications.

## Editorial voice

Write in clear, direct language for a reader who is curious about Scripture and how translation works. Explain the idea before introducing specialist vocabulary. Describe historical people, practices, languages and decisions in concrete terms.

**Do not write slogans.** This includes slogans used as headings, motivational catchphrases, rhetorical one-liners, exaggerated contrasts, or a catchy phrase followed by a second catchy phrase. A heading should state what the section actually discusses.

**Prefer headings like:**
- How Hebrew and Aramaic word roots affect translation
- How the Aramaic-speaking setting informs our reading of the Greek Gospels
- Why we render “reign” in Matthew 4:17
- How the translation process and research are documented
- Intellectual sources and influences

**Avoid headings like:**
- The words matter. So does the world behind them.
- A word belongs to a world.
- Unlock the hidden message.
- Not just a translation. A revolution.

## Our main perspective

The Way Version examines Scripture within the language, imagery, relationships and cultural assumptions of the people who first heard it. Languages carry much more than a list of dictionary meanings.

In Hebrew and Aramaic, the root-and-pattern formation of words joins expressions into families of associated meaning. Traditions of interpretation, including Jewish midrash, revisit related roots, sound, wordplay, whole passages and—in some streams—the shapes and symbolic possibilities of individual letters. This is a distinctive, layered approach to sacred language. Those particular relationships are not automatically carried over when the wording moves into Greek, Latin or English.

Neil Douglas-Klotz makes this case in [“Midrash and Postmodern Inquiry”](https://abwoon.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/10/Midrash.pdf), presented at the Society of Biblical Literature in 1998 and published in 1999, as well as in [*Prayers of the Cosmos*](https://abwoon.org/prayers-of-the-cosmos/) and his other books. We draw on his **method of asking what a Semitic-speaking audience heard**, rather than copying his renderings or making him the authority for our own English choices.

## How to write each explanation

1. **Begin with the recognizable passage or lived experience.** Debt, bread, sowing, family, breath, land, travel, time and community are more useful introductions than abstract terminology.
2. **Explain the ancient language.** Show a specific word, root, idiom or pattern and how it connects to other uses in its linguistic setting.
3. **Reconstruct the first audience's world.** Ask how relationships, trade, work, poverty, law, empire, Scripture, prayer or geography might shape the hearing.
4. **Explain what happened in translation.** Show what the Greek, Latin and English versions emphasize and which associations are harder to carry into English.
5. **State TWV's actual editorial choice.** Quote the current wording; link the verse. Where the project chooses a poetic expression, say how it extends the source wording in one natural sentence.
6. **Link sources and invite inquiry.** Connect directly to the [reader](https://thewayversion.com/app/), [About](https://thewayversion.com/about/), relevant [GitHub research](https://github.com/Wayback-Project/thewaytranslations), or a specific issue.

## Examples and conventions

- **Matthew 6:12:** `opheilēmata` / debts; discuss real obligations, reciprocity and restoration within first-century communities.
- **Matthew 4:17:** `basileia` / kingdom or reign; explain the choice of “reign” with the text and Jewish expectations of rule.
- **John 3:8:** `pneuma` / wind, spirit; include the Hebrew `ruach` and Aramaic `ruḥa` family where context is relevant.
- **Matthew 6:9:** “Cosmic Parent” develops the relational image of “Father”; identify it as TWV's interpretive expansion without laboring the point.
- **John 1:1:** “Living Creative Vibration” is TWV's expression of creative speech; explain its literary image and the Greek `logos` word that informs the discussion.
- **Time:** Discuss Hebrew verbal forms and Douglas-Klotz's caravan image of ancestors and descendants as a way of understanding continuity across generations.
- **Letters:** Be specific about the role of individual letters in **interpretive traditions**; do not invent a new obligatory dictionary meaning for every letter in an ordinary word.

## Research and attribution

Use [Neil Douglas-Klotz's scholarly paper](https://abwoon.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/10/Midrash.pdf) as a primary methodological reference. Credit the contributions of Martin Buber, Shulamith Hareven, Richard Horsley, Hanson and Oakman, Abraham Rihbany, George Lamsa, George Kiraz and other scholars when their work is actually used. Author references identify intellectual influence; they do not suggest endorsement of TWV.

Use intellectually confident prose. Do not bury our interpretive perspective under defensive framing, repeated “not this, not that” disclaimers, or generic false balance. Keep a clear record of the source word and the interpretive English choice in editorial notes; accuracy and reader confidence are compatible.

**No-slogans rule applies to all headings, taglines, buttons, repository prose and future marketing drafts.**
